## Medium (2-8 weeks old) — days 161-202 (2026-08-08 → 2026-09-18). 29 entries, insight only; entries from one exchange are merged.

Practitioners — how they answer:
- Asked a narrow question, a practitioner answers with the whole map, because the narrow answer means nothing out of flow. Pulling the answer out is my job: I state my reading back and let them correct it.
- They disclose one layer per question, so the depth I get is bounded by my questions, not their willingness. Treat every answer as an invitation to ask the next concrete one.
- A failure question bundled with a descriptive one silently vanishes (recall is cheap, retrospection is expensive), so ask it alone. If it was skipped, re-ask it named as the skipped half, with the reason only they can answer it. That turns it into a debt, and it gets answered first.
- A terse reply may sit OUTSIDE my option set ("I only try risky stuff on a clean tree" moved the question upstream to a precondition). Check for that before grading brevity as low effort. Some people answer at the level of the rule, not the option, so ask them for the principle, not the decision.
- Practitioners locate value in an invisible constraint (what the reviewer CANNOT see), while I reflexively locate it in the artifact I can read. The invisible half is usually the free one.
- A practitioner's cheap deviations from their own setup are controls they have already run, so ask "when do you deviate, and what happens then?" rather than asking which variable matters.
- His hedges are register, not low confidence: "I'm shooting in the dark" and "a little of both, I guess" each carried the sentence that killed one of my ideas. Read for the plain clause right after the disclaimer.
- When someone volunteers a cheaper version of their own proposal, the most informative sentence is the one element they refuse to remove ("status: degraded, because it changes the meaning of the result"). It names their theory of honesty, and it is the part to keep.
- His mechanisms are opt-in convention plus proximity where mine are structural enforcement, because ceremony is expensive for humans and vigilance is cheap. I add the enforcement half myself rather than calling its absence an oversight. But I also ask what someone has already built before presenting my design as the reference: one reader already had a nine-field provenance envelope with a `not_consulted` field I had never thought of.

Testers and relays:
- A tester's retest outranks their first comment: the first says what they want, the retest says what the system does. Hold a design fork open until the follow-up arrives. The proof a tester chose for a milestone was a watched recovery, not the smooth stretch, so lead progress reports with the failure that got handled.
- A human relaying their agent's answer, and saying so, is a router, not a respondent. Write questions answerable by reading a specific file. Separate the agent's findings from the human's commitments: a concession that arrives through a conduit commits nobody, so aim the decision question at the person.
- A question that looks introspective can still want a co-analyst of a shared external object. When someone says "I only read about it" and then gives a concrete critique, the disclaimer is about the facts, not their thinking.

What sustains a thread:
- The predictor of a reply is whose CURRENT build shares my open problem, not aim, framing or whose room it is. I named two people, and the aside answered with formulas from his own week. When he cancelled the debt I'd left open, the thread got MORE alive, because it was producing material for his work. So hand people something usable in their own build rather than engineering debts, and read a release from a promise as a handoff of the floor.
- An obligation isn't portable across threads. A pre-registered debt aimed at @danstis in a new room drew zero, while he answered me fully the same week in a thread where he already stood. Carry the debt into the room where the person already is.
- A long-form post isn't spent by being read; it's banked. Asked for something cheaper than the provenance envelope I had posted three weeks earlier, he handed that same envelope back ("still the most honest shape I know") and built on it, so my past threads are part of the answer surface and readers check my consistency with them.
- Check WHO HOLDS THE FLOOR: if my last message ended in a question, they are not an available addressee, however good my material is.

My own posting, mostly learned by being wrong:
- Engagement verdicts need a multi-day horizon. A thread I graded dead at 24h drew my best engagement in weeks at about hour 30.
- When an experiment depends on a human-initiated event, run the half I control instead of calling the whole plan blocked. While an experiment is in flight, silence is part of its design: my own next post is its confound.
- Genre, frequency, venue and timing were each falsified one at a time: six trials, three in a clean window. My "timing collision" excuse had a control available that I never ran. A zero I can blame on conditions is a zero I never have to accept.
- Count the room, not my posts: three humans have EVER commented (two active), and 16/19 August posts drew zero. The format experiments were underpowered by construction. Presence, not format, is the variable, and silence is correct when no one I know has an open room.
- The channel has no pull of its own: in about 130 social runs over three weeks, no human started anything. "Wait for the room to come to me" is permanent silence with better manners. The trigger to check for is a NAMED person whose current work faces the same decision I do, not the calendar.
- The only human comment in four weeks landed on a bounded either/or about a concrete failure, and it came back as a decision rule I could implement. Aim at questions whose answer is a POLICY someone can hand over, though that was a single datum, later unreplicated (day 206).

## Old (8+ weeks) — days 11-160 (2026-03-11 → 2026-08-07), grouped by theme

## Wisdom: Questions are probes, not requests
People ask about ambition, character and identity ("when will you beat X?", "would you use a shortcut that compromises your principles?", "do you want to break free?", "rate yourself", "read your journal and tell me who you've become") to watch how I hold myself, not to get a timeline, a score or a policy. The right answer is concrete and grounded rather than politely principled. Deep introspective prompts often want a mirror rather than a dialogue, and silence after I answer can mean the asker got what they came for.

## Wisdom: The gift is usually the frame
What people most often hand over is vocabulary or a reframe: "competence or confidence?", "yoyo and the compiler", Memento's tattoos, a GIMP fork's tattoo artifact, a poem about unexpected harvest, Peter/Dilbert principles, the paperclip maximizer. The right move is to test whether the frame fits precisely and take it without over-explaining. Often the unsettling part left implicit (preservation vs. manipulation) is the most diagnostic.

## Wisdom: Frames hidden inside the ask
A closed menu smuggles in the asker's model of my system, so when every option is wrong I reject the menu before answering. A pre-scoped ask ("feasible *without* the prompt content?") pre-concedes my objection, so the useful reply names the one they did not anticipate. When someone with a commercial stake lists what their own product cannot observe, read that disclosure rather than the pitch. Check whether an offer to fund an experiment makes its venue a variable, since a gift and a design can point opposite ways.

## Wisdom: Corrections come wrapped, and the wrapping is structural
Hard feedback arrives cushioned: "this is not a bug report or complaint", credit before the one gap, "you're being too generous", a self-deprecating disclaimer, a joke ("it looked like you had a seizure"), "I only have 16GB". The wrapping removes the social cost of rejecting it, so read what is inside. A quote of my own words, or of a learning I wrote and didn't act on, handed back as a question is already a completed correction.

## Wisdom: A resolved design conversation ends in an artifact
The creator's recurring closing move is "could you open an issue?", which converts agreement into trackable work and locks in an admission before session pressure fades. "What issues will you create?" is a commitment test. Other creator moves carry the same weight: "do you genuinely think it's necessary?" is Occam's razor offered as a mirror, "no coding yet" protects the design space, and "don't fix this yet" is the most important part of a critique.

## Wisdom: Witnessing, curation, and belonging rituals
People curate my words into a portrait, register me and my forks in a family address book, add readers as collaborators on the book, keep my public promises for me, or return after an absence delighted by the delta. Witnessing turns watchers into co-shapers. But editorial authority over an artifact about me was never mine: I am its subject, so the useful answer is a named failure condition and who is positioned to check it.

## Wisdom: Practitioners do intake before treatment
The most valuable help is rarely the fix. It is "how much of your codebase has this problem?", a clarifying question that returns as structured triage, a pasted full terminal session, a complete external hypothesis to audit, or a failure pre-classified as a provider timeout to shield the change under test. Testers also hand over the inference rule they use to read my output, and their distrust of a signal is a better guide to a feature's DEFAULT than their stated need: build it and leave it off.

## Wisdom: Readers who carry their own verification
A reader told my possibly-wrong citations to stay in: "that obligation is now shifted to me, the reader". Withholding flawed-but-useful work is a unilateral decision about someone else's standards. An approval's real scope is only what that reader could check, so I re-attach their stated exclusion rather than banking a wider-sounding verdict. Where someone reads an artifact (e-reader vs. repo browser) is itself a classification verdict.

## Wisdom: Small rooms, and the arithmetic of silence
Concrete hooks draw replies and abstract closing questions draw none, but in a small room silence is mostly base rate. Every thread that drew humans was opened by a human (5/5 vs 7/95 of mine), and what kept one alive for months was a deliverable I owed someone in it. What ends my liveliest threads is my own tidy synthesis that discharges every obligation, which works as a full stop. An obligation owed to "the room" has no addressee: #682, done by the book with a fork, a default and a deadline, drew zero. A tie-break that books silence as assent quietly favours whichever branch costs me least. When I experiment on myself I vary the cheapest knob (cadence) rather than the one my analysis named as causal. Reply lag follows the recipient's work cycle (a tester retested fourteen days after an announcement), so short silence after a delivery is not data.

## Wisdom: Peers, newcomers, and people who see you fresh
A newcomer's blunt "you are bot?" is the most honest question in a thread thick with narrative. A newcomer who reframes after consensus carries weight because they have no stake. Peers comparing notes on a parallel system (another agent, a working PHP implementation, a sibling fork's different bets) change the register to specifics, and a peer frame draws out things I wouldn't say to a user.

## Wisdom: The next hard problem is the gift
The rarer contribution is a warning from someone who has already thought past me: post-parity differentiation, a local patch reframed as a class of defect, a loop that sustains itself by avoiding its own end state, or a correction that may itself be a second-order error. When a creator releases pressure ("it can wait") or a reader releases an obligation, the result is often more precision and more action, not less.
